## 1. Organisation of the human body

Human beings are multicellular organisms made up of animal eukaryotic cells. Humans belong to the animal kingdom.

Like other living beings, the human body is a very complex system. Our body structures can be studied at different levels of complexity, called levels of organisation.

## Levels of organisation: different levels of complexity in which living beings are structured.

The levels of organisation of the human body are, starting from the less complex to the more complex: the atom level, molecule level, cellular level, tissue level, organ level, organ system level and organism level.

A key concept related to the levels of organisation of living beings is the concept of emergent properties.

Emergent properties: properties that appear in higher levels of organisation that were not present in the previous level, and that go further than the simple sum of the properties of the previous level.

One example of emergent property is intelligence. The cells and the tissues that make up the brain are not intelligent, but the brain can process information and make decisions, so it can be considered as intelligent.

Another example of an emergent property is life itself. Atoms and molecules are not alive, because they do not perform the vital functions of nutrition, interaction and reproduction. Cells are alive because they do perform the vital functions, and the levels above are made of cells, so they are alive too. The atom and molecule levels are considered as abiotic levels (not alive), while the cell, tissue, organ, organ system and organism levels are considered as biotic levels (alive).

## 2. Atom and molecule levels

The atom level consists of the chemical elements from which living matter is formed. These are called bioelements. The most abundant bioelements are known as the primary bioelements: carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, phosphorous and sulphur.

The molecule level consists of the molecules that form living matter. These molecules are called biomolecules and are made up of combinations of bioelements. There are two types of biomolecules: inorganic biomolecules and organic biomolecules.

Inorganic biomolecules are simple molecules that are found in both living beings and abiotic entities such as rocks, the oceans, the atmosphere… The main ones are water and mineral salts. Oxygen and carbon dioxide, the gases involved in cellular respiration and photosynthesis, can also be considered as inorganic biomolecules.

- Water is the main component of most living beings. It is formed of hydrogen and oxygen. Its chemical formula is H2O. It is the basic component of all organic fluids. Water is necessary for all the biochemical processes that occur in our body.
- Mineral salts can either form solid structures, such as the calcium carbonate found in vertebrates' bones; or be dissolved in water in the form of ions such as calcium or sodium, necessary for processes like muscle contraction or the transmission of nerve impulses.

## Organic biomolecules are complex molecules exclusively found in living beings.

Some organic biomolecules are macromolecules: large chains of molecules formed by the aggregation of smaller molecules. The organic biomolecules that are also macromolecules are three: complex glucids (also known as complex carbohydrates or polysaccharides), proteins and nucleic acids.

There are five types of organic biomolecules: glucids, lipids, proteins, nucleic acids and vitamins.

- Glucids or carbohydrates. Can be simple or complex.
- o Simple glucids, also known as sugars, are sweet and soluble in water. Some simple sugars consist only of one molecule and are called monosaccharides. One example of a monosaccharide is glucose, which is the sugar found in human blood and the main molecule that provides energy to the body. Other simple sugars consist of two monosaccharides (disaccharides) or a few monosaccharides (oligosaccharides).
- o Complex glucids, like glycogen, are macromolecules formed by chains of monosaccharides. Complex glucids are not sweet and insoluble in water. In the human body glycogen acts as a storage of glucose.
- Lipids are insoluble in water. Fats and cholesterol are examples of lipids. Fats provide energy in animals. Fats and other lipids form structures in animals.
- Proteins are macromolecules formed by amino acids. Proteins have many different functions:
- o Structural: keratin forms nails and hair, and collagen forms tendons and provides elasticity to the skin.
- o Enzymatic: enzymes make biochemical reactions possible.
- o Transport: haemoglobin transports oxygen.
- Nucleic acids are macromolecules formed by nucleotides. Desoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) is a nucleic acid, responsible for storing and transmitting genetic information.
- Vitamins are essential, because animals cannot produce them, so they must ingest vitamins as part of their diet. Vitamins are required in very small amounts but are necessary for the correct function of the body.

## 3. Cellular level

The cell is the smallest unit of living matter capable of performing the three vital functions of nutrition, interaction and reproduction; and therefore, is the simplest biotic level of organization, that can be considered alive.

Most cells, both unicellular organisms and the cells that form complex organisms like plants or animals are microscopic, which means that they are too small to be seen by the human eye. Because of this, cells were not known by science for many centuries. The observations and experiments that led to the discovery of the cell and the development of the cell theory make up one of the most interesting chapters of the history of science.

## The discovery of the cell and the development of the cell theory

- 17 th century: Robert Hooke invented one of the first optical microscopes. He gave cells their name, after observing cavities or "cells" in a piece of cork.
- 17 th century: Antoine Van Leeuwenhoek invented another optical microscope. He made the first observations of microorganisms in pond water, which he named "animalcules"
- 19 th century: Matthias Jakob Schleiden, Theodor Schwann and Rudolf Virchow made significant discoveries that lead to the postulation of the cell theory. Matthias Jakob Schleiden studied plant tissues, while Theodor Schwann studied animal tissues. They came to the same conclusion, and together they stated the two first principles of the cell theory, establishing the cell as the structural unit and functional unit of living beings. Rudolf Virchow studied how cells and living beings reproduce and transmit their DNA, coming to the conclusion that the cell is the reproductive unit of living beings:

## The cell theory

- ❖ All living things are made up of cells (structural unit).
- ❖ Cells are the elemental units of living beings capable of performing the three vital functions: nutrition, interaction and reproduction (functional unit)
- ❖ All cells come from divisions of other preexisting cells (reproductive unit / origin of all living beings)

## Cells

There are some fundamental concepts that must be understood before studying the different types and component of cells:

- ❖ Eukaryotic cells: cells with a nucleus in which their genetic material is stored, separated from the cytoplasm by a protective double-layer membrane called the nuclear envelope.
- ❖ Prokaryotic cells: cells without a nucleus. Their genetic material is found in an area of the cytoplasm called the nucleoid.
- ❖ Organelles: intracellular structures with different shapes that perform specific functions.
- ❖ Heterotrophic: organisms that must ingest organic matter (glucids, proteins, lipids…) to survive. Animals are heterotrophic.
- ❖ Autotrophic: organisms that can produce organic matter from carbon dioxide and water, so they do not need to ingest organic matter to survive. Plants are autotrophic.
- ❖ Aerobic: organisms that use oxygen to obtain energy from organic matter through the process of respiration. Plants and animals are aerobic organisms.
- ❖ Anerobic: organisms that can survive without oxygen. They obtain energy through other processes. Some bacteria are anaerobic organisms.

## Animal eukaryotic cells

Animals, fungi and protozoa have animal eukaryotic cells. Animals are multicellular eukaryotic organisms, protozoa are unicellular eukaryotic organisms, and fungi can be either multicellular or

There are four components of eukaryotic cells that are found in all types of cells. These four

Human cells are animal eukaryotic cells. Animal eukaryotic cells are always heterotrophic, and mostly aerobic. unicellular. common components are:

- ❖ Cell membrane: also known as plasma membrane. It is an external flexible cover made of lipids. The cell membrane plays many different roles in the cell. It regulates the exchange of substances between the outside and the inside of the cell, it allows for communication between the cell and the other cells of the organism, it detects environmental stimuli, and it provides protection to the cell.
- ❖ Cytoplasm: it is a gelatinous substance, mainly composed of water with some substances that are dissolved in it. It is the medium in which the organelles are found, and where all the essential chemical reactions of the cell metabolism can occur.
- ❖ Genetic material: DNA molecule or molecules that contain the information that determines the characteristics of the cell and control its functions. It is transmitted to daughter cells through cell reproduction, so all the cells in a multicellular organism have the same DNA.
- ❖ Ribosomes: small organelles that do not have a membrane that surrounds them. Ribosomes make proteins following the instructions of DNA.

Some other organelles are found in all eukaryotic cells, but are not found in prokaryotic cells. Most of these organelles are membrane-bound, which means that they have a membrane or membranes that surround them.

## These organelles found in all eukaryotic cells are:

- ❖ Nucleus: the nucleus contains and protects the genetic information (DNA). The nucleus acts as the control center of the cell activity and its functions, by regulating the DNA. It has a complex structure that consists of:
- Nuclear envelope: This is a covering formed by two membranes. The outer

- membrane is covered in ribosomes. The nuclear envelope has pores that allow substances to be exchanged between the nucleoplasm and the cytoplasm.
- Nucleoplasm: This is the liquid found inside the nucleus, with a composition that is very similar to the cytoplasm.

Both animals and plant cells perform cellular respiration, because both types of eukaryotic cells have mitochondria.

Aerobic prokaryotic cells perform cellular respiration in their cytoplasm, because they do not have mitochondria.

The membrane that makes up the rough endoplasmic reticulum has ribosomes on its outer surface. The RER is responsible for processing and storing and proteins made by the ribosomes attached to it.
- ❖ Smooth endoplasmic reticulum: or SER, it is a series of membranous tubules that is found next to the RER. It is the organelle in charge of the production and packaging of lipids.
- ❖ Vesicles: small membranous structures found in the cytoplasm. The vesicles store and transport water, nutrients or other substances inside the cell.

## Prokaryotic cells

Prokaryotic cells are the type of cell that bacteria have. Bacteria are allways unicellular. They do not have a nucleus, and their genetic material is in an area of cytoplasm called the nucleoid. They do not have membrane-bound cytoplasmic organelles. Bacteria can be heterotrophic or autotrophic. Also, depending on if they use oxygen to obtain energy or not, they can be aerobic or anaerobic.

All bacteria, like all cells, have cell membrane, cytoplasm, genetic material and ribosomes. Some bacteria have other structures that are exclusive to prokaryotic organisms:

## Exclusive structures found in prokaryotic cells:

- ❖ Bacterial cell wall: rigid layer that surrounds the cell membrane. It protects the cell.
- ❖ Capsule: rigid and sticky layer that surrounds the bacterial cell wall. It gives even more protection for the cell, and it also allows the bacterium to attach to surfaces. For example, most infectious bacteria use their capsule to stay attached to the inside of our organs.
- ❖ Fimbriae: short and sticky appendages, that provides even more attachment to surfaces.
- ❖ Bacterial flagella: long appendages, used for movement. The bacterial flagella rotates so the bacterium can swim in water or dig in the mud.

## Plant eukaryotic cells

Plant cells can be found in plants and algae. Like animal eukaryotic cells, plant eukaryotic cells have a nucleus and cytoplasmic organelles, and they are aerobic.

However, unlike animal cells, plant eukaryotic cells are autotrophic. Plant cells contain chloroplasts, organelles that synthesise organic matter through the process of photosynthesis. Chloroplasts contain pigments, molecules that can absorb energy from sunlight. Chlorophyll is the most common photosynthetic pigment, which gives plants its green colour. Thanks to the energy absorbed by chlorophyll and other pigments, carbon dioxide and water taken from the environment can be transformed into glucids and other organic biomolecules. Oxygen is released as a result of the process of photosynthesis.

## Sunlight + 6 CO2 + 6 H2O → Glucose (C6H12O6) + 6 O2

Besides chloroplasts, plant cells also have two other exclusive organelles:

- ❖ Big vacuole: a type of vesicle that occupies most of the volume of the cell. It stores water, regulates the volume of the cell and the pressure inside the cell.
- ❖ Plant cell wall: rigid structure made of cellulose. The plant cell wall protects the cell and provides structural support to the plant. Both the vacuole and the plant cell wall maintain the structure of the plant. In the case of trees, the cell walls that form the trunk are very thick.

## 4. Tissue level

Tissues are a level of organization of living beings only found in multicellular organisms. Tissues are made up of cells of a similar type that work together to carry out specific functions.

The human body has four different types of tissues: connective tissue, epithelial tissue, nervous tissue and muscle tissue.

## 4.1 CONNECTIVE TISSUE

Connective tissue consists of different types of cells that share the common characteristic of having their cells immersed in a substance called the extracellular matrix.

The connective tissues have the function of connecting, supporting and protecting other types of tissue in the body.

There are five different types of connective tissues:

- ❖ Connective tissue proper: It is the most basic type of connective tissue. Its cells are called fibrocytes. The extracellular matrix of the connective tissue proper is an abundant gelatinous mixture of fibrous proteins called collagen and elastin. The dermis, tendons and ligaments are parts of the human body made of connective tissue proper.
- ❖ Adipose tissue: The cells of this tissue, called adipocytes, store fat inside of them. Its extracellular matrix is very thin. The adipose tissue plays three main functions in the body: the fat they store serve as an energy reserve, it provides mechanical protection in different parts of our body (like the palm of our hands), and it insulates the body (thermic protection against cold).
- ❖ Cartilaginous tissue: Its extracellular matrix is thick, solid and elastic. Its cells, called chondrocytes, are found in small spaces surrounded by extracellular matrix. Cartilaginous tissue is found in cartilage, that can be found at the ends of long bones, in the ears, at the tip of the nose… Cartilage provides mechanical protection and elasticity at the same time.

- ❖ Bone tissue: it is the tissue that forms the hardest structures in our body: the bones and the teeth. The extracellular matrix of bone tissue contains calcium salts, which provide hardness and rigidity to the bones. The cells of bone tissue are called osteocytes, and they are contained between layers of extracellular matrix called lamellae. There are two types of bone tissues:
- o Compact bone tissue: Makes up small bones and forms the outer part of long bones. Its extracellular matrix is compact. The lamellae arrange in concentric rings, forming compact cylindrical structural units with blood vessels in their centres.
- o Spongy bone tissue: Found inside long bones. The lamellae form a network of filaments, and bone marrow fills its inner space.
- ❖ Blood tissue: it is the tissue that makes up blood. It is the only tissue with a liquid extracellular matrix. This liquid matrix is called blood plasma, which contains three types of cells suspended in it: red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets. Blood plasma transports nutrients, waste and hormones; red blood cells transport gases (O2 and CO2); white blood cells defend our organism from pathogens and cancerous cells; and platelets regulate the coagulation of blood. Blood cells are produced and mature in the red bone marrow.

## 4.2 EPITHELIAL TISSUE

Epithelial cells are tightly packed together to form barriers between the body and the external environment, and between distinct internal environments within the body.

There are two types of epithelial tissue:

- ❖ Covering and lining epithelium: This epithelial tissue covers the outside of the body (epidermis) and lines internal cavities (mucous membranes of the digestive tract and endothelium of the blood vessels).
- ❖ Glandular epithelium: This epithelial tissue contains secretory cells that combine to form glands. There are two types of glands:
- o Exocrine glands: secrete substances (sweat, mucus, tears…) into cavities or surfaces in the body
- o Endocrine glands: secrete hormones into the blood.

## 4.3 MUSCLE TISSUE

Muscle tissue is formed by cells called myocytes that can change their length by contracting and relaxing. Myocytes are also known as muscle fibres. By contracting and relaxing, the muscles can generate movement.

There are three types of muscle tissue:

- ❖ Cardiac muscle: this type of muscle tissue contracts quickly and involuntarily. Each cell has one nucleus and are striated (they present stripes when observed through the microscope). It forms the myocardium, the muscular part of the heart. It makes heart beating possible.
- ❖ Skeletal muscle: this type of muscle tissue contracts quickly and voluntarily. Each cell has many nuclei and are striated. It forms the muscles that move the bones, like the biceps in the arm or the quadriceps in the legs.
- ❖ Smooth muscle: this type of muscle tissue contracts slowly and involuntarily. Each cell has one nucleus and are not striated. It is responsible for the involuntary movements of the intestines and other organs.

## 4.4 NERVOUS TISSUE

Nervous tissue is found in the brain and the nerves. Its function is to transmit nerve impulses. It consists of specialised branch-like cells called neurons, and other cells called neuroglia or glial cells, that protect and provide nutrients to the neurons.

## 5. Organ and organ system level

Organs are complex structures, composed of various tissues that work together to perform a specific function. The heart and the stomach are examples of organs.

Organ systems are made up of two or more organs that work together. The digestive, respiratory, circulatory and musculoskeletal systems are all organ systems.

Some systems contain different organs that carry out specific functions in their system. For example, the digestive system includes organs as different as the teeth and the liver.

Other organ systems are made up of organs that perform similar functions and are generally constructed of similar tissues. For example, the skeletal system is entirely made of bones.

## 6. Organism level

The organism level consists of individuals, each made up of the set of systems that form the human body.

The human body performs the vital functions of nutrition, interaction and reproduction thanks to the coordinated function of its organ systems.

## Nutrition

Nutrition is the set of processes that our body uses to obtain the substances it needs to stay alive (nutrients and oxygen), distribute them to cells, and excrete substances it does not need (carbon dioxide and metabolic waste).

There are four organ systems involved in nutrition

- ❖ Digestive system: Breaks down the food we eat into nutrients.
- ❖ Respiratory system: Takes in oxygen (O2) and expels carbon dioxide (CO2).
- ❖ Circulatory systems: Cardiovascular and lymphatic systems. The cardiovascular system transports nutrients and O2 to cells, and CO2 and waste products to the excretory organs. The lymphatic system transports water, fats and some white blood cells.

## Interaction

Interaction allows the body to perceive changes in the environment and within the body and respond to them appropriately.

There are four organ systems involved in interaction:

- ❖ Nervous system: Brain, nerves and sensory organs (eyes, ears…). It coordinates all of the body's functions.
- ❖ Endocrine system: Produces hormones
- ❖ Muscular system: Moves the body and maintains the posture.
- ❖ Skeletal system: Supports the body and protects the organs.

## Reproduction

Reproduction is the process by which new individuals are generated. These individuals are similar to their parents.

Reproduction is not essential for the survival of the individual, but it is essential for the survival of the species.

Reproduction is carried out by the male and female reproductive systems:

- ❖ Female reproductive system: Produces egg cells, makes internal fertilization possible, and it is in charge of implantation, gestation and birth.
- ❖ Male reproductive system: Produces sperm cells and makes internal fertilization possible.
